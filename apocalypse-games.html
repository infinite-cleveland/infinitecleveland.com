Timelines 67 and 68: The Day Baseball Broke

In 69 simulations of Game 3 of the 1997 World Series, the average combined score is about 13 runs. Most games look like baseball games. Some are pitching duels. Some are sloppy. Some are blowouts.

Timelines 67 and 68 are not baseball games.

Timeline 67 ends 23-22. Sixty-three combined hits. Forty-five total runs.

Timeline 68 ends 25-24. Sixty-five combined hits. Forty-nine total runs.

Together these two games produce 94 runs, 128 hits, and zero pitching performances that could be described as anything other than catastrophic. Every single pitcher on both rosters — all fourteen of them — gives up runs. Every reliever gets shelled. Both starters get shelled. The closers get shelled. The long men get shelled. The setup men get shelled. There is no escape.

These are the games at the far edge of the probability distribution, the timelines where everything that can go wrong for a pitcher goes wrong simultaneously, for every pitcher, on both sides, for nine straight innings.

Timeline 67: The 23-22 Game (W, CLE 23-22 FLA)

Al Leiter takes the mound for Florida to start the game, throws 28 pitches, and gives up 7 runs without recording a single out. Six hits and a walk. He doesn't survive the 1st inning. He doesn't survive the 1st half of the 1st inning.

This sets the tone for what follows.

Inning by Inning

1st inning: FLA 7, CLE 2. Florida scores 7 off Charles Nagy in the top half. Cleveland answers with only 2 — including the Jim Thome grand slam that drives in 4 — before Leiter's implosion ends the inning. After one full inning, it's 7-6 Florida. Already 13 runs have scored. Nobody has settled in.

2nd inning: FLA 1, CLE 4. Cleveland takes its first lead of the game, 10-8, hitting everything Jay Powell throws. It won't last.

3rd inning: FLA 1, CLE 0. A brief moment of quiet. Florida 9, Cleveland 6 after 3 complete.

4th inning: FLA 1, CLE 3. Cleveland chips away. 10-9 Florida.

5th inning: FLA 7, CLE 4. Dennis Cook comes in for Florida and immediately surrenders 7 runs on 7 hits while recording exactly one out. He faces 10 batters. Cleveland gets 4 in their half. The score is 17-13.

6th inning: FLA 0, CLE 3. Cleveland keeps clawing. 17-16. The margin is 1 run.

7th inning: FLA 0, CLE 0. The only scoreless inning of the entire game. A miracle.

8th inning: FLA 2, CLE 6. Cleveland explodes. Six runs. They take the lead for the first time since the 2nd inning. It's 22-19 Cleveland. After 8 innings of chaos, Cleveland is on top.

9th inning: FLA 4, CLE 0. Robb Nen takes the mound for Cleveland to close it out. He can't. Florida scores 4 in the top of the 9th to take back the lead 23-22. Cleveland goes down 1-2-3 in the bottom of the 9th. After all of that — after 63 hits, after blowing open the game and clawing back and taking the lead in the 8th — Cleveland loses by one.

Timeline 68: The 25-24 Game (W, CLE 25-24 FLA)

If Timeline 67 is chaos, Timeline 68 is despair.

Florida leads this game from the first pitch to the last. They never trail. Not for a single half-inning. And yet Cleveland scores 24 runs trying to catch them and never does. This is the Sisyphean timeline — the one where you push the boulder up the hill for nine innings and it rolls back down every time.

Inning by Inning

1st inning: FLA 4, CLE 1. Florida jumps out to a quick lead. Normal enough.

2nd-3rd innings: FLA 1, CLE 0. The calm before the storm. Florida 5, Cleveland 1.

4th inning: FLA 6, CLE 6. Both teams detonate simultaneously. Six runs apiece. After the 4th, it's 11-7. Every pitcher who enters the game in this inning leaves it worse than they found it.

5th inning: FLA 0, CLE 0. The eye of the hurricane.

6th inning: FLA 5, CLE 3. Florida pulls away. Felix Heredia gives up 5 runs on 5 hits for Cleveland without recording an out. The score is 16-10.

7th inning: FLA 3, CLE 0. The lead swells to 9. 19-10. The game should be over.

8th inning: FLA 0, CLE 6. It's not over. Cleveland scores 6. Paul Assenmacher comes in for Cleveland and gives up 5 runs on 5 hits without getting an out — but that was in the 6th. The 8th is where Cleveland's bats catch fire. 19-16.

9th inning: FLA 6, CLE 8. This is where it gets cruel. Cleveland needs 3 runs to tie. They don't get 3. They get 8. Eight runs in the bottom of the 9th. But Florida put up 6 in the top of the 9th first, making it 25-16 before Cleveland started hitting. Even an 8-run 9th inning rally — the kind of inning that defines an entire postseason — isn't enough. Cleveland scores 8 and loses by 1.

The Pitching Carnage

  • Al Leiter (FLA): 3 IP, 10H, 6R, 6ER, 3BB. His best outing of the two Apocalypse Games.
  • Felix Heredia (FLA): 0 IP, 5H, 5R, 5ER. 22 pitches, no outs, five earned runs.
  • Charles Nagy (CLE): 3 IP, 11H, 7R, 7ER. 88 pitches of punishment.
  • Michael Jackson (CLE): 0 IP, 5H, 5R, 5ER. Five batters faced, five hits surrendered.
  • Paul Assenmacher (CLE): 0 IP, 5H, 5R, 5ER. A mirror image of Jackson.
  • Robb Nen (FLA): 0 outs recorded, 4H, 3R, 3ER. Again.
  • Jaret Wright (CLE): 0 IP, 6H, 3R — but somehow gets credited with the win. The OOTP gods work in mysterious ways.

The Hitters

  • Bobby Bonilla (FLA): 5-for-8, 2 RBI

  • Edgar Renteria (FLA): 5-for-6, 3 RBI, 4 runs

  • Craig Counsell (FLA): 2-for-7, 5 strikeouts. Struck out 5 times and scored 2 runs, because when 49 runs are scoring, even the guys flailing at air end up crossing the plate.

The Combined Wreckage

Across Timelines 67 and 68:

  • 94 total runs scored
  • 128 combined hits
  • 14 pitchers used; all 14 gave up runs
  • 0 pitchers posted a game ERA below 6.00
  • Cleveland scored 8 runs in the 9th inning in both games and lost both
  • Moises Alou went a combined 11-for-14 with 13 RBI

Somewhere in the multiverse, October 21, 1997 is the day that baseball stopped making sense. In these two timelines, the starting pitchers combined to last 7 innings between them. The closers combined for 0 outs recorded and 14 runs allowed. Every rule about what a baseball game looks like — how many runs score, how many pitchers you need, how long a game takes — was suspended.

And in both games, Cleveland came back, mounted furious rallies, scored enough runs to win any normal game three times over, and lost by one. Both times. By one.
